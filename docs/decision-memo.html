The plan needs 400 letters. Dispersal is short by two orders of magnitude, and no amount of extra paper closes it. There are three compounding reasons, and each is worse than it sounds.

The wall the cards point at is empty

The seventy-two seeded letters were deleted in the 4 September audit — app/src/wall/seed.js now holds card attribution and nothing else. So every one of those thousand scans lands on a wall with almost nothing on it, searches their own name, and finds their handle printed under the word nobody.

Search-to-found across campus is 0.79%

Even at the plan's own 400-letter target, spread over 33,122 undergraduates, a person who searches has a four-in-five-hundred chance of finding themselves. To hit a 20% found-rate campus-wide you need roughly 23,600 letters. Inside a house of 60, you need fifteen.

And the loop can't start itself

No letters means nobody finds anything, which means nobody writes, which means no letters. The cold-start fixed point is zero at every concentration. It does not begin on its own. Someone has to sit down with twenty people and ask them to write, by hand, before any card moves.

Every operator who has done this — Bier, Marriage Pact, Tinder, Fizz — began by making one enumerable group who see each other daily adopt on the same day, by hand. Your plan has no such moment anywhere in it.

02

Three things to stop this week

Stop
Paying creators to say it's trending
This is the one with a number attached. The FTC's fake-reviews rule (16 CFR 465, in force since October 2024) covers buying or disseminating testimonials you know to be false, at a civil penalty of more than $50,000 per violation ($51,744 when the rule took effect, adjusted for inflation each year) — plausibly per video. It is also self-detecting: twenty paid posts in one week on one campus where everyone shares group chats gets identified as astroturf, and the debunk post travels further than the campaign. And your own VOICE.md §4 already bans it by name as “the pattern the FTC took NGL apart for.” Write the prohibition into the repo permanently.
Stop
Dispersing the cards around campus
Section 01 is the arithmetic reason. There's a second: it's against Berkeley's regulations as written. Nothing may be left unattended on campus grounds or in classrooms, nothing affixed to doors — which is cafeteria tables, library carrels and under dorm doors, in order. The remedy (becoming a registered student org) takes a full semester and the next deadline is 1 December.
Stop
The 200-person party, for this autumn
Not deferred vaguely — cancelled, with the venue search, the insurance quotes and the $6,000 float. Its ceremony engine is unbuilt, the mutual maths needs three letters each aimed at people in the room, and a plan that opens with an unfunded $3,925 loss on night one is a wish. It comes back twice, small, in section 05.
Also stop
Tristan's wall of printed handle-addressed letters
As designed it breaks your own eighth gate: paper on a wall outlives the one-tap takedown, so the tap becomes a lie. This is the best idea in the plan and it comes back in November made of published, de-named letters — which is lawful, and is better footage.

Everything above is reversible today and expensive next month. None of it is a judgement call about taste — two of the three have statutes or regulations behind them.

03

The cards: $65 and they're fine

They aren't wasted. The sunk cost is $25–80, which buys them no claim on the strategy — but the QR route behind them is genuinely good infrastructure.

One · Tell the truth

“I miss you, I wrote a letter for you” is a first-person claim from a sender who doesn't exist. It's false for roughly 998 of 1,000 holders, and the modal experience it creates is the worst four seconds this product can produce: a stranger says they miss you, you search your own name, and you find nothing.

A roll of matte stickers · about $24

I miss you, I wrote a letter for you, check it out

letters people never sent. berkeley.

celestual.us — search your @

True for 100% of holders. And a second sticker for the cards handed inside a saturated house, where the original claim actually becomes true: somebody wrote a letter for you. That version is your Named Card, printed — and it's only honest once the house has been seeded.

Two · Repoint, don't reprint

One commit in app/src/cards.js. The repo built the /c/<code> hop for exactly this — paper can't be redeployed, so the destination is one word in one file. Assign one code per population, not per creative variant:

  • c/a house one · c/b house two · c/c hand-to-hand on Telegraph and Bancroft (city sidewalk, not campus grounds) · c/d the reading · c/e held

Migration 0047 already logs scan → read → asked → joined per card. Per population, inside a known denominator, that gives you a conversion table nobody in this category has ever published. Five creative variants at this volume is statistically inert — 1,000 cards can't resolve two variants, let alone five.

Three · Hand them, never leave them

About 150 inside house one, 100 beside the reading in November, 300 hand-to-hand across the autumn, and roughly 450 stay in the box until the RSO application clears in spring. Paper doesn't expire.

04

The best idea in here is one database field

Three independent analyses arrived at the same place: what makes your letters unspreadable isn't the letters. It's the address line.

Here's the structural diagnosis of why this whole category dies. Does a piece of content have any value to a reader it doesn't name? A Yik Yak post does. An Unsent Project message does. A Gas poll does. A letter addressed to @sarahkim03 does not — it's interesting to exactly one person on earth, so it can't travel, and network effects never form.

But that's a property of one column, not of the content. Your own composer already proves it. These are the example letters shipping in Write.jsx right now:

Already in the product

You gave me your umbrella outside Wheeler and walked home in it. I still have it.

You were the one singing on the 51B that night. I wanted the song to be about me.

A thousand people have stood outside Wheeler in the rain. The body already carries its own address. The handle is a separate field.

The fifty / one rule

Every published letter is addressed to a description that at least fifty people could believe is them, and no more than one can prove is them.

That's the Unsent Project's mechanic exactly — enough to self-identify, not enough to resolve — and it's enforceable by a regex plus one classifier pass, not by judgement. It goes into VOICE.md as a section, into WALL-FEATURES.md as a tenth gate, and into the voice linter.

But do not drop the handle from the product

This is where I'd push back on the most aggressive version of the idea. Two reasons, both load-bearing:

  • Your one cryptographic proof depends on it. wall_claim authorises the whole claim → reveal-request → sealed-line dialogue on exactly one condition: the verified handle matches the letter's target. Null the target and that becomes an assertion any verified stranger can make about any letter, aimed at a writer you promised anonymity.
  • Nobody searches for a building. “Search your own @” is the entire funnel and the only free reach channel you have. Self-interest is what makes a stranger open the site.

So: the handle is composed, and never published. Written, then rewritten at publication. Two objects, one corpus — and that is one column and one editorial pass, not a redesign.

05

The party — answered directly

You asked three real questions. Here they are in order.

“Would people even care about a stupid app when they're out to party?”

No. They would not. And that's the design constraint, not the objection. People go out because their friends are going and because someone they're interested in will be there. Nobody has ever gone to a party for software.

Which means the app has to be invisible. Any moment where someone is looking at a phone is a design failure. What Celestual can uniquely do isn't entertain people at a party — it's tell one specific person somebody here wrote about you, which is a reason to attend that no other event can manufacture.

“How do we make it not cringe?”

Cringe comes from one thing: the event being about the product. Compare Marriage Pact — nobody says “the Marriage Pact app,” they say “did you get your match.” The noun is the ritual, not the software. So:

  • Never name the night after the company. It's a reading, or it's a house dinner. Celestual supplied the material.
  • Attending must be deniable. Your whole product is built on deniability; the event needs the same property. “Someone wrote about me” is conferred status and reads as a flex. “I'm here looking for love” is the cringe version. The invite framing does all the work.
  • Students read other people's letters aloud. This is the Mortified / PostSecret Live mechanic and it's the whole unlock: reading someone else's words frees the reader from shame and frees the room to laugh and ache without anyone being exposed.

“How do we get people to come on a weekday?”

You're not competing with a better party — Greek parties are Friday and Saturday. You're competing with study hours, and an open-ended evening prices at an unknown number of them. So:

Put the end time in the headline, not the start time. “tuesday, 8:00–9:15. you'll be back at the library by half nine.”

Nobody in campus marketing writes the end time as the promise, which is exactly why it reads as a real thing said by a real person. And then actually stop at 9:15.

So what actually runs

Early October · ~$150

The house night

40–70 people at their own dinner, in their own lounge. No venue, no insurance, no Type 48 problem, no door, no cover. Dinner as normal, ninety seconds of explanation, everyone writes three letters aimed at people in the room — then everyone searches their own handle at the same time. That's the ceremony, and it needs zero new code.

November · ~$150

The reading

Forty people, a bookshop back room or a co-op common room. A hundred published, de-named letters on the walls. Strangers read them aloud to each other. One camera. Its output is fifteen minutes of footage of real people reading real letters — the highest-value asset this plan can manufacture.

Kill the simultaneous count reveal. This corrects a recommendation from my own earlier plan. A private number delivered at the same second to people standing next to each other becomes a public comparison within seconds — and a public zero for everyone it misses. The per-name count is already on the public index, so nobody in that room learns anything they couldn't have learned alone on a Tuesday. Pairs only, or nothing.

Two free things the plan is missing

  • Get paid to show up instead of paying to show up. An RSO partner applies to ASUC or SUPERB or residence-hall programming for $400–1,500 to host a student-written letters reading. This is exactly how PostSecret Live has run at hundreds of campuses for two decades — the programming board books it, the room and the insurance are the institution's problem. It flips the night from a loss to break-even and solves the room, the flyering and the tabling in one application.
  • The Big Game bonfire rally queue. Friday 20 November, Hearst Greek Theatre, roughly 10,000 students with forty minutes of nothing to do. Four people carrying an 8ft letter wall down that queue is more impressions than 3,000 cards, it's free, and it produces the one shot no paid creator can fake: strangers stopping to read strangers' letters. Stay on the public sidewalk approach.
06

The macro call: two branches, one engine

You asked whether to pivot. The honest answer is that what's broken is smaller than a pivot and bigger than a campaign.

Branch A · clock: weeks

The product, concentrated

The wall exactly as built. Handle-addressed, .edu gate, three letters in five days, thirty-day lapse, nine gates. Nothing about the product changes. Only the population: from 33,122 to one bounded group with a roster, a room and a recurring meal.

Branch B · clock: years

The publication, a by-product

One letter a day, handle stripped, address rewritten as place and moment, published permanently. Not a separate company and not 70% of your engineering — a hundred-line extension of export-og.mjs plus two hours a week of editing.

The relationship between them is the design. A produces B's material. B produces A's reach, and B's only call to action is “the wall is at celestual.us/berkeley — search your @.” A decays on purpose, one population at a time. B never decays.

The answer to “it's a growth decay business”

You're right, and it isn't fixed by pivoting. Four things are true instead:

  • Decay is a property of a mechanic applied to a population, not of a company. Campus-wide you get one novelty clock and then all of Berkeley is post-novelty forever. House by house, there are seventeen co-ops, sixty-odd chapters and dozens of floors — each with its own clock that starts when you walk in. You don't fight the curve. You buy a new one every few weeks for $150.
  • The thirty-day lapse is the calendar, not a bug. A house saturates on night one and empties thirty days later, and its own monthly meal is the rewrite trigger.
  • The corpus doesn't decay, because it names nobody. The lapse exists to protect a named non-consenting person. Strip the name and permanence is free. Branch A's asset base shrinks every day by design; Branch B's only grows. That asymmetry is the only real answer anyone has to decay — the response isn't a better growth plan, it's an asset whose value isn't a function of growth.
  • You get to schedule the peak instead of suffering it. The novelty peak is 6–12 weeks and unavoidable. Don't spend it in autumn 2026 with both founders 5,500 miles away and $60 in the bank. Spend the military window accumulating a corpus and one working room playbook, and detonate the peak in autumn 2027 with a founder physically in the room.

Your own framework reached this independently, and it's worth quoting back: “content is the only loop that works at zero density, which makes it the correct primary activity during the military window.” You wrote that. You're not doing it.

One operational fact nobody has used

Korea is 16 hours ahead of Pacific. Berkeley's peak risk hours — 7pm to 10pm — are 11am to 2pm in Korea. The moderation desk can be staffed live by a founder, from a barracks, at no cost. The constraint you've been treating as the problem is an advantage on this one axis.

What I'm still unsure about

Whether a room self-feeds once seeded. Nobody knows this, for any product in this category. It's Gate 3 and it's the whole question.

Whether campus-addressed letters travel outside the campus, and whether they pull Berkeley specifically. Two different questions. The Unsent Project is both the proof and the warning here: eleven years, five million submissions, and zero density anywhere. “Become a smaller Unsent Project” is not a strategy, and typography is not a differentiator. Your only real difference is that your corpus has a place — the 51B at 11pm, the fourth floor of Moffitt — which is a smaller audience but a convertible one.

The reciprocity rate. It's the most interesting number this product will ever learn, and it is not worth building for this autumn — measuring it needs the reveal function, and the simultaneous search is already a ceremony that fires for nearly everyone with zero new code. Build it in November only if Gate 1 passes.

What Ace does with the other 2,700 engineering hours. This is the honest weak point of every version of this plan. The finite list — 0046, the ceiling, the linter, the licence, the renderer — is maybe 250 hours. Don't spend the rest adding features to the wall: every feature it doesn't have is a feature that can't be turned on somebody. Name the real risk out loud to each other now, because it isn't the ambassadors quitting. It's two founders with idle evenings drifting away from a codebase that has nothing urgent in it.

Twelve months out, honestly: about 50% that three or four rooms have run, 500–900 Berkeley students have written or been written about, the account is 3,000–25,000 across four surfaces, and a book has sold 150–600 copies — revenue $1,500–5,000 against ~$4,000 of burn. About 15% that something breaks out. About 35% that it doesn't carry and you stop, having spent under $1,500 and four months of evenings.

Your own framework put “durable multi-year company” at 5–10%. Nothing here moves that much — call it 8–12%. What changes is the floor. Under the plan you're running now the downside is $6,000 gone, three burnt-out ambassadors, an FTC exposure and a false claim on a thousand cards. Under this one the downside is a published archive, a mailing list, a playbook, and a very specific piece of knowledge about whether people write letters to each other when nobody is asking.
